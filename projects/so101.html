/projects/so101

SO-101 robot arm: from teleoperation to a learned grasp

Five stages over one semester: get the arm moving, model it in simulation, solve its kinematics, run pick-and-place on the real robot, then train a reinforcement learning policy to do the grasp itself.

Course
ME 297 Embodied Intelligence & Robot Learning, SJSU, Fall 2026
Robot
SO-101 6-DOF arm (LeRobot), leader + follower pair
Stack
Python, MuJoCo, NumPy, Stable-Baselines3, LeRobot, MATLAB Simscape
Outcome
IK pick-and-place on hardware. SAC lift policy: 10/10 evaluation episodes.

01

Build, calibrate and teleoperate

Get the real leader and follower arms working before writing any control code.

What I built

I set up the SO-101 leader and follower arms with Hugging Face's LeRobot library, calibrated each joint's range, and teleoperated the follower by moving the leader by hand. The leader uses lower-reduction servos so it moves easily by hand, while the follower's are stronger. Alongside that, I imported the arm's URDF into MATLAB Simscape to see the kinematic chain move before touching the real robot.

Concepts

  • Leader–follower teleoperation
  • Servo calibration
  • Serial-bus servos
  • URDF
  • Simscape Multibody
02

A MuJoCo twin of the arm

A physics simulation I could break safely, commanded the same way as the real robot.

What I built

I loaded the SO-101 MJCF model into MuJoCo and wrote the layer between how the real robot is commanded (joint angles in degrees, gripper 0–100) and what MuJoCo's position actuators expect (radians). On top of that I wrote move_to_pose, which interpolates linearly in joint space over a set time while stepping the physics in real time, and hold_position. Every later stage reuses these two functions.

Concepts

  • MJCF models
  • Position actuators
  • Joint-space interpolation
  • Real-time physics stepping

Challenges and fixes

  • problem

    The real robot's API and MuJoCo's actuators use different units, so every script risked mixing degrees, radians and gripper percentages.

    fix

    One conversion module used everywhere. Simulation and hardware scripts take the same pose dictionaries, so a pose tested in sim runs unchanged on the arm.

03

Forward kinematics and scripted pick-and-place

Know exactly where the gripper is for any set of joint angles, then use that to plan a pick-and-place.

What I built

Forward kinematics as a chain of 4×4 homogeneous transforms, one per joint. Each link's fixed offset and orientation come straight from the body position and quaternion in the MJCF file, followed by a rotation about that joint's z-axis. The chain ends at the gripperframe site between the fingers.

T_gripper = T_shoulder · T_upperArm · T_lowerArm · T_wrist · T_gripper · T_tip
T_i       = [ R(q_i)·Rz(θ_i)   p_i ]      q_i, p_i read from the MJCF
            [ 0  0  0          1   ]

I used it to draw ghost target cubes exactly where the gripper would close, then scripted grasp → lift → transit → lower → release in simulation and ran the same joint sequence on the real arm.

Concepts

  • Homogeneous transforms
  • Quaternion → rotation matrix
  • Kinematic chains
  • Waypoint sequencing

Challenges and fixes

  • problem

    The FK reference point sits on the fixed fingertip, so a target cube drawn exactly there didn't sit between the jaws.

    fix

    Shifted each target 1 cm outward along the arm's pan direction, which lines it up with the closing jaws.

  • problem

    Moving straight from the pick pose to the place pose would sweep the gripper low across the table.

    fix

    Added safe waypoints: lift to shoulder level, pan across, then lower at the place pose.

04

Closed-form inverse kinematics

Go from "put the gripper here" to joint angles with geometry instead of an iterative solver.

What I built

A geometric solver for a top-down grasp, solved one joint at a time:

  1. Shoulder pan (θ₁): project the target onto the ground plane, measured from the pan axis, which sits 3.9 cm in front of the base origin.
  2. Wrist position: for a top-down grasp the wrist sits directly above the target by the gripper's length, 16.5 cm.
  3. Shoulder lift and elbow (θ₂, θ₃): law of cosines on the shoulder–elbow–wrist triangle. The upper arm has a 2.8 cm kink, handled as a fixed angle offset.
  4. Wrist flex (θ₄ = 90° − θ₂ − θ₃): keeps the gripper pointing straight down.
  5. Wrist roll (θ₅ = θ₁): keeps the jaws aligned with the block.

I tested it against random targets in simulation, then wrapped it in pick and place routines (hover, descend, close, lift, then the reverse at the drop point) and ran them on the real arm.

Concepts

  • Geometric IK
  • Law of cosines
  • Workspace analysis
  • Checking IK with FK
  • Sim-to-real validation

Challenges and fixes

  • problem

    Random test targets outside the arm's reach pushed the law-of-cosines term past 1. arccos returned NaN, MuJoCo received NaN commands and flagged the simulation as unstable.

    fix

    Reachability became part of the IK call: a NaN solution means unreachable, so the target is rejected and the arm keeps its last command.

  • problem

    Checked against forward kinematics, the analytic IK missed the requested point by about 0.7 cm in x and 1.0 cm in z.

    fix

    A 3-iteration correction loop: solve IK, use FK to see where the gripper actually lands, shift the request by the error, solve again.

  • problem

    Close to the base, the solution asks the wrist to bend past its joint limit, and the actuator silently clamps it.

    fix

    Bounded the working area (x ≥ 10 cm, z ≤ 10.5 cm). Clamping inside the IK was tried and tilted the gripper, so it was left out.

05

Reinforcement learning: grasp and lift

Replace the scripted sequence with a policy that learns to grasp a 3 cm cube and hold it above 8 cm.

Attempt 1: my own environment

I wrote a Gymnasium environment around my own IK. The agent outputs a small Cartesian move (dx, dy, dz) plus open or close, the IK turns that into joint commands, and a 13-number observation gives the gripper position, the cube's position and orientation, and the vector to the grasp point. I trained it with SAC from Stable-Baselines3.

Before any training, a hand-coded expert policy ran through the same environment to prove the task was solvable. That caught physics problems that would otherwise have looked like the RL failing.

Challenges and fixes

  • problem

    The agent learned to close the gripper early and press down on top of the cube.

    fix

    A gripper gate: closing is only allowed at the grasp point beside the cube, or while already holding it.

  • problem

    It also collected reward for corner pinches and for cubes wedged above the fingers.

    fix

    A "good grasp" test: both jaws touching, cube upright (tilt under 20°), faces aligned (yaw error under 15°), centred between the pads. Lift rewards only pay out on a good grasp.

  • problem

    Even a correct grasp slowly let the cube creep out of the jaws.

    fix

    Swept six contact settings using the scripted expert. An elliptic friction cone, impratio 10, 5 no-slip iterations and full 6-D contact on the cube held it.

  • problem

    The policy learned to end episodes early on purpose. Failures were flagged as time-limit truncations, so SAC kept bootstrapping value through them and quitting cost nothing.

    fix

    Made failures real terminal states with a −5 penalty. Only the step limit counts as a truncation.

  • problem

    In one 700k-step run the policy "picked" the cube with the fingers passing through it, which is a contact-geometry problem, not a learning one.

    fix

    For the final training I moved to an environment with explicit finger-pad collision boxes.

Final run: a tuned environment

For the final policy I trained SAC on the open-source pick-101 SO-101 lift environment. It has finger-pad collision geometry, a dense staged reward, a top-down grasp with the wrist locked, and episodes that start with the gripper open just above the cube. I wrote a faster training config: 700k steps instead of 1M, evaluation every 10k steps, checkpoints every 25k, and a smaller replay buffer. Training took about 3 hours on my laptop.

Concepts

  • Soft Actor-Critic
  • Reward shaping
  • Gymnasium environments
  • Terminated vs. truncated
  • Contact physics tuning
  • Curriculum start states
  • Observation normalization

/results

Results

The arm went from hand-driven to a learned grasp-and-lift in one semester, with a hardware test at each kinematics stage.

10/10evaluation episodes lifted the cube above 8 cm and held it, at 700k steps
~120ktraining steps before an evaluation round first hit 100% success
~3 hto train 700k steps on a laptop

Success is measured over 10 deterministic evaluation episodes. Averaged across the later evaluations, success sits around 90%, so a single 10/10 round shouldn't be read as perfect reliability.
